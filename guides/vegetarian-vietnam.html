Vegetarian food in Vietnam: a practical guide for Indian travellers

Updated September 2026

Vietnam is easier for vegetarians than many people expect. The country has a long Buddhist vegetarian tradition called chay, and every city has quán chay (vegetarian eateries). The catch: ordinary dishes often hide meat broth, fish sauce or shrimp paste. This guide gives you the words to show, the dishes to order and the traps to avoid.

1. The one word to learn: “chay”

Chay (sounds like “chai”) means vegetarian. Look for it on signs and menus: cơm chay (vegetarian rice), phở chay, bún chay, nhà hàng chay (vegetarian restaurant). On Google Maps, GrabFood or ShopeeFood, simply search “chay” to see nearby options.

Many Vietnamese Buddhists eat vegetarian on the 1st and 15th day of the lunar month, so chay places are especially busy (and have the most choice) on those days.

2. Phrases to show on your phone

VietnameseMeaning
Tôi ăn chay.I am vegetarian.
Không thịt, không cá, không tôm.No meat, no fish, no shrimp.
Không nước mắm.No fish sauce.
Không trứng.No egg.
Không hành, không tỏi.No onion, no garlic (for Jain diets).
Nước dùng chay không?Is the broth vegetarian?

Tip for Jain travellers: strict Vietnamese Buddhist vegetarian cooking often avoids onion and garlic too, so a dedicated quán chay is usually your safest choice. Root vegetables are still common, so ask.

3. Dishes that are usually safe (in a chay restaurant)

  • Cơm chay – rice with a choice of vegetable and tofu dishes, served buffet-style. Point at what you want.
  • Đậu hũ sốt cà chua – fried tofu in tomato sauce. A comfort dish close to home cooking.
  • Phở chay / bún chay – noodle soup with vegetable broth.
  • Gỏi cuốn chay – fresh rice-paper rolls with tofu and herbs. Ask for soy or peanut dip, not fish sauce.
  • Bánh mì chay – baguette with tofu or mock meat. Check that no pâté or egg is added.
  • Thịt chay – “vegetarian meat”: soy or gluten mock meat. It looks real; that is intended.

4. Watch out for

  • Broth: regular phở and bún broth is made from beef, pork or chicken bones, even if there’s no visible meat.
  • Nước mắm (fish sauce): in dressings, stir-fries and dipping sauces. Morning glory (rau muống) is often cooked with it.
  • Mắm tôm (shrimp paste) and dried shrimp in salads.
  • Oyster sauce in stir-fried vegetables.

5. What it costs

A vegetarian rice plate at a local chay restaurant is typically 30,000–55,000 ₫. Use the Dong Helper converter to see it in rupees, and its “Fair price?” tab to check any quote.

6. Missing Indian food?

Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City and Da Nang all have Indian restaurants, mostly near the backpacker and tourist streets, many with pure-veg menus. Search “Indian vegetarian” on Google Maps and check recent reviews. Prices are higher than local chay food, so mixing both keeps the budget under control.
